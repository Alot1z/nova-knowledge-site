Beating the bandits without a feedback loop

The head-to-head that should have gone the other way is the bandit comparison.

Contextual bandits exist to solve exactly this problem: pick among arms, observe the reward, update. They get to learn from the stream while they are being evaluated. PIMO is static — one checkpoint, no online update, no exploration — and it won:

The recorded decision was PIMO WINS 5/5 on test regret, 5/5 on unseen regret, 5/5 on test accuracy. Every regret comparison, on both splits, against every bandit.

Why this is the interesting result

A static model beating an adaptive one is not the usual story, and the usual explanation — "the bandit had not converged" — is weak here. LinUCB variants are near-optimal in the linear setting they are specified for, and this is a linear reward model. They had the whole stream.

The explanation is structural. A disjoint linear bandit maintains an estimate per arm and spends its exploration budget discovering arm-specific reward parameters. That works when arms are fixed and rewards are linear in known features. It degrades on two things this world punishes:

Arm identity is the wrong parameterisation. The bandit learns "arm 7 is good in this region". PIMO learns "an arm with this cost/latency/risk/quality/availability profile is good in this state shape". The unseen-template split separates these: bandit regret rises from 0.0252 to 0.0257 and 0.0124 to 0.0144 across the split, while PIMO's falls, from 0.0029 to 0.0019. A model keyed by identity has to meet a new identity; a model keyed by properties does not.

Exploration is a cost the world charges you. linucb_disjoint_greedy and linucb_disjoint differ by roughly one exploration term and land within 0.0005 regret of each other. At small regret that difference is noise; it tells you the exploration tuning is not where the loss is. The loss is in the parameterisation.

Three fairness rules, written before the run

An adaptive-versus-static comparison is easy to rig by accident, so the rules were written into the experiment file:

  1. Same features. Every bandit sees the same 30-dimensional state and 28-dimensional option

vectors as the full model. A bandit given only aggregates should lose, and would have.

  1. Same telemetry. Every method records outcome, latency and health through the same registry

feed. No method got a private, richer signal stream.

  1. Same split, same temperature protocol. Temperature is fitted on held-out data for every

method, not hand-set for the model under test and left alone for the baselines.

Rule 1 is the one that is most often quietly violated. "We compared a bandit to a transformer" usually means "we compared a bandit with a 4-feature summary to a transformer with the full state". The interesting comparison is equal information, unequal parameterisation.

The honest counterweight

There is a regime where this result does not hold, and it is not a corner case.

Non-stationarity. The world here is stationary by construction, which is exactly the condition bandit literature says to be suspicious of. A world where the online provider degrades at hour 18, or where a competitor ships a cheaper model on Tuesday, breaks the assumption that makes a static checkpoint safe. In that regime, an adaptive method's whole reason for existing is live, and a static checkpoint is a liability: it will keep routing to an endpoint that stopped working.

Cold start. The bandits start from a prior and learn; PIMO starts from a checkpoint trained on a world that may not resemble yours. If your traffic distribution differs sharply from world-analytic-v1, the first thousand decisions are all bandit, and the regret curve starts high.

Both objections have the same answer and it is the same answer the project gives for cost and latency: feed back real telemetry, and the priors become observations. record_outcome, record_latency, record_health exist for exactly this, and gate A11 is open until they are used on real traces.

There is also a straight architectural answer, listed in the design document as future work: a properly adapted PIMO — the same architecture with online updates to the heads — should beat a static PIMO the same way it beat a static bandit. The comparison in EXP-007 is a lower bound on the architecture, not a ceiling.

What to take from it

Not "deep learning beats bandits". Bandits are still the right answer when arms are fixed, rewards are linear, and the world moves — that is what the regret bounds are for, and EXP-007 records that LinUCB gets to 0.0252 where a naive rule gets 0.1152.

Take instead: **when the candidate set is open and rewards depend on option properties rather than option identity, a per-instance scorer beats per-arm estimation, and the gap widens on templates it has never seen.** That is a claim about parameterisation, it is measurable, and it is the claim PIMO actually makes.
